<p>To better understand the master Josquin des Prez (c 1450/1455-1521) and his unique style, the Renaissance vocal ensemble Cut Circle, under the direction of Stanford professor Jesse Rodin, explores a well-documented period (1489-1494) in his life in this 2-cd set. The recording is centered around music Josquin probably wrote in Rome, but for comparison also includes world-premiere pieces by contemporaries (all singers in the Sistine Chapel Choir in the late 15th century), one of whom is Marbrianus de Orto (c. 1460-1529), the other less-featured composer.</p>
<p>The tracks are nicely organized, lending themselves well to comparison. We are immediately immersed in Josquin’s style, with a hymn and excerpts from two masses that open the first disc. A diverse selection of pieces by Gaspar van Weerbeke follows. His <em>“Dulcis amica dei</em><em> / Da pacem”</em> is particularly beautiful. An anonymous setting of the Credo acts as a palate cleanser – it employs primarily homophonic writing – and is a welcome change of pace. Music by de Orto follows, with a Glora setting from his <em>Missa Ad fugam.</em> Two settings of “<em>Domine non secundum peccata nostra</em>,” the first by Bertrandus Vaqueras, the second by Josquin, conclude the first disc. Vaquerara’ setting is very active and ornate.</p>
<p>The second disc features two masses based on the popular “<em>L’homme armé</em>” melody. If you are unfamiliar with the melody, it is considerately included as a brief second track on the disc. De Orto’s <em>Massa L’Homme armé</em> is relatively unknown, and contrasts with Josquin’s more famous setting. I find de Orto’s setting (and the rest of his pieces on the recording) refreshing. He employs a slightly more exotic sound world than Josquin. De Orto’s “<em>Ave Maria mater gratie</em>” is absolutely stunning and nicely sung one to a part. Just like the Credo in the first disc, the “<em>Factum est silentium</em>” chant provides a calm break and prepares us for Josquin’s Missa <em>L’homme armé</em>, which concludes the second disc. The CD concept and selections are excellent – it’s not always easy to find music that is both unrecorded and interesting.</p>
<p>Cut Circle uses only one or two singers to a part on this recording – the best way to deliver the clarity required for Renaissance polyphony. Rodin, a professor at Stanford University and a specialist in 15th-century music, interprets these pieces perfectly. The tempos and shaping are spot on and bring to life this great music, which is too often recorded and performed with a lack of vitality. The ensemble is tight, and each line of polyphony is sung clearly and expressively. The basses and sopranos sound particularly good, both parts executing with a great sense of ease and body in the sound. The booklet is a work of art and is translated into found languages – French, Dutch, English, and German. The engineering and production are very good, striking a wonderful balance between presence and ambience. This recording is a great addition to any music collection.</p>
